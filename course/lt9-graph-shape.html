    Worksheet Learning Target 9: Increasing, Decreasing, and Concavity
    

    Objectives

    • I can identify the intervals where a function is increasing, decreasing, concave down, or concave up, and I can provide examples of familiar functions that have given increasing / decreasing and concave down / concave up behaviors.
      🔗
    🔗

    1. Problem 1: A Sinusoidal Function.

    The graph of \(\displaystyle T(x) = -2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}x\right) + 1\) is shown below on the domain \([0, 8]\text{.}\) The dots mark the minimum and maximum.
    🔗
    
    The graph of T starts at the point zero, one, falls to a minimum at two, negative one, rises through four, one to a maximum at six, three, and falls back to eight, one.
    🔗

    (a)

    On which intervals is \(T\) increasing?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The graph rises from the minimum at \(x = 2\) to the maximum at \(x = 6\text{,}\) so \(T\) is increasing on \((2, 6)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    On which intervals is \(T\) decreasing?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(T\) is decreasing on \((0, 2)\) and on \((6, 8)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What is the midline of \(T\text{?}\) Find every \(x\)-value in \([0, 8]\) where the graph crosses the midline.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The midline is \(y = 1\text{.}\) The graph crosses it at \(x = 0\text{,}\) \(x = 4\text{,}\) and \(x = 8\text{.}\) On a sinusoidal graph, the concavity changes each time the graph crosses the midline.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    On which intervals is \(T\) concave up? On which intervals is \(T\) concave down?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Below the midline the graph bends like a cup, and above the midline it bends like a frown. So \(T\) is concave up on \((0, 4)\text{,}\) where the graph is below \(y = 1\text{,}\) and concave down on \((4, 8)\text{,}\) where the graph is above \(y = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Problem 2: Exponential Functions.

    Each function below has the form \(f(x) = c + ab^x\text{.}\) Without graphing, decide whether the function is increasing or decreasing, whether it is concave up or concave down, and whether \(f(x)\) approaches a value \(c\text{,}\) \(\infty\text{,}\) or \(-\infty\) as \(x \to \infty\text{.}\) Then check your answers with Desmos.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(P(x) = 4(1.2)^x + 10\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Here \(b = 1.2 \gt 1\text{,}\) so \((1.2)^x\) grows, and \(a = 4\) is positive. The function is increasing and concave up, and \(P(x) \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(Q(x) = 50 - 6(2)^x\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Here \(b = 2 \gt 1\text{,}\) but \(a = -6\) is negative, which flips the growing curve upside down. The function is decreasing and concave down, and \(Q(x) \to -\infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(R(t) = 12(0.6)^t - 5\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Here \(b = 0.6 \lt 1\text{,}\) so \((0.6)^t\) decays toward \(0\text{,}\) and \(a = 12\) is positive. The function is decreasing and concave up, and \(R(t) \to -5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    \(S(t) = 98 - 60e^{-0.4t}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Since \(e^{-0.4t} = \left(e^{-0.4}\right)^t\) and \(e^{-0.4} \approx 0.67 \lt 1\text{,}\) this is decay, and \(a = -60\) flips it. The function is increasing and concave down, and \(S(t) \to 98\text{.}\) This is the shape of a warming model, like a cold drink approaching room temperature.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    \(V(t) = 20 + 8e^{0.3t}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Since \(e^{0.3} \approx 1.35 \gt 1\text{,}\) this is growth, and \(a = 8\) is positive. The function is increasing and concave up, and \(V(t) \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Write your own exponential function of the form \(c + ab^x\) that is decreasing, concave down, and approaches \(-\infty\) as \(x \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Answers will vary. We need \(b \gt 1\) so the function grows in size, and \(a \lt 0\) to flip it upside down. One example is \(f(x) = 7 - 2(3)^x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Problem 3: A Cubic Function.

    The graph of a function \(f\) is shown below on the domain \([-4, 4]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    The graph of f starts at the point negative four, negative three, rises to a local maximum at negative two, five, falls through the point zero, one to a local minimum at two, negative three, and rises to the point four, five.
    🔗

    (a)

    On which intervals is \(f\) increasing?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(f\) is increasing on \((-4, -2)\) and on \((2, 4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    On which intervals is \(f\) decreasing?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(f\) is decreasing on \((-2, 2)\text{,}\) from the high point down to the low point.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    At \(x = -3\text{,}\) is the graph concave up or concave down? Explain.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Concave down. Near \(x = -3\) the graph is part of the hill that peaks at \(x = -2\text{,}\) so it bends like a frown. It is rising, but more and more slowly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    At \(x = 3\text{,}\) is the graph concave up or concave down? Explain.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Concave up. Near \(x = 3\) the graph is part of the valley with its low point at \(x = 2\text{,}\) so it bends like a cup. It is rising, and more and more quickly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Problem 4: Quadratic Functions.

    Consider the two quadratic functions \(p(x) = 5 - 2x^2\) and \(q(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Is \(p\) always concave up or always concave down? What about the formula tells you this?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Always concave down. The coefficient of \(x^2\) is \(-2\text{,}\) which is negative, so the parabola opens downward.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Is \(q\) always concave up or always concave down? What about the formula tells you this?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Always concave up. The coefficient of \(x^2\) is \(3\text{,}\) which is positive, so the parabola opens upward.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the vertex of \(q\text{.}\) Then give the intervals where \(q\) is increasing and where it is decreasing.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vertex is at \(x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-12}{2(3)} = 2\text{,}\) and \(q(2) = 12 - 24 + 1 = -11\text{,}\) so the vertex is \((2, -11)\text{.}\) Since the parabola opens upward, \(q\) is decreasing on \((-\infty, 2)\) and increasing on \((2, \infty)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Give the intervals where \(p\) is increasing and where it is decreasing.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vertex of \(p\) is at \(x = 0\text{,}\) the point \((0, 5)\text{.}\) Since the parabola opens downward, \(p\) is increasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and decreasing on \((0, \infty)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Problem 5: Sketching from a Description.

    (a)

    On the grid below, draw a possible graph of a function \(f\) such that:
    🔗
    • the graph can be drawn without lifting your pencil (continuous) on \([-6, 6]\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(f(-6) = -3\text{,}\) \(f(0) = 4\text{,}\) and \(f(5) = -2\) (plot these first),
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(f\) is increasing on \((-6, 0)\) and decreasing on \((0, 6)\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(f\) is concave up on \((-6, -3)\text{,}\) concave down on \((-3, 3)\text{,}\) and concave up on \((3, 6)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    
    A blank coordinate grid with x and y axes, running from negative six to six in each direction.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Answers will vary. Starting at \((-6, -3)\text{,}\) the graph rises slowly at first and then more steeply, bending like a cup until \(x = -3\text{.}\) It keeps rising but starts to level off, bending like a frown, and reaches its highest point at \((0, 4)\text{.}\) It then falls, still bending like a frown, more and more steeply until \(x = 3\text{.}\) After \(x = 3\) it keeps falling but levels out, bending like a cup, and passes through \((5, -2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    At what \(x\)-values does your graph change concavity?
    🔗
    Answer.
    At \(x = -3\) and \(x = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Problem 6: Examples of Familiar Functions.

    For each description, give a formula for a familiar function with that behavior. Sketch a small graph to support your answer. There is more than one correct answer.
    🔗

    (a)

    Increasing and concave up for all \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Answers will vary. One example is \(f(x) = 2^x\text{,}\) which rises more and more steeply.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Decreasing and concave up for all \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Answers will vary. One example is \(f(x) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^x\text{,}\) which falls and levels off toward \(0\text{,}\) like a cooling model.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Increasing and concave down for \(x \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Answers will vary. Examples include \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) and \(f(x) = 10 - 5(0.5)^x\text{.}\) Each rises, but more and more slowly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Concave down for all \(x\text{,}\) increasing for \(x \lt 1\text{,}\) and decreasing for \(x \gt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Answers will vary. A downward-opening parabola with its vertex at \(x = 1\) works, such as \(f(x) = -(x - 1)^2 + 3\text{.}\)
